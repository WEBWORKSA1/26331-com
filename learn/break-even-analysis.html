Break-even analysis in 10 minutes

Planning · 6 min read · Updated November 2026

Break-even is the point where revenue exactly covers costs: no profit, no loss. It is the most useful single number in a business plan because it turns a vague goal (“be profitable”) into a concrete target (“sell 39 coffees a day”).

The formula

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price per unit − variable cost per unit)

The bottom of that fraction is the contribution margin: the amount each sale contributes toward fixed costs once its own direct costs are paid.

  • Fixed costs stay the same whatever you sell: rent, salaries, insurance, software, loan payments.
  • Variable costs rise with each sale: ingredients, packaging, card fees, delivery commissions.

A worked example: a small café

Monthly fixed costs$9,000
Average ticket$12.00
Variable cost per ticket$4.20
Contribution margin$7.80 (65%)

Break-even = $9,000 ÷ $7.80 = 1,153.8, so the café needs 1,154 sales a month, or about 39 a day over 30 days. In revenue terms that is 1,154 × $12 = $13,848 a month.

Plug in your own numbers with the break-even calculator.

Using break-even to make decisions

Testing a price rise. Raise the average ticket to $13 and the contribution margin becomes $8.80; break-even drops to 1,023 sales — 131 fewer customers needed each month.

Testing a new hire. A $3,000-a-month employee raises fixed costs to $12,000. At $7.80 per sale, the café now needs 1,539 sales. The question becomes: will this person help generate about 385 extra sales a month?

Testing a supplier change. Cutting variable cost by 50 cents lifts contribution to $8.30 and lowers break-even to 1,085 sales.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the owner’s own pay is the big one — if you need to draw a salary, it is a fixed cost. Using list price instead of the average price actually paid after discounts is another. Finally, break-even assumes costs behave neatly; at very high volumes you may need extra staff or space, so recalculate in steps.

The bottom line

Know your break-even, post it where your team can see it, and recalculate whenever prices, costs or headcount change. It turns every decision into a simple question: does this move the line up or down?
